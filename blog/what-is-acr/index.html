What is ACR? A control room for every application you run

October 13, 2026 · 5 min read
ACR team
Application Control Room

Most teams run production with two separate toolboxes. One tells you what is happening: analytics, an error tracker, a log viewer, maybe an uptime pinger. The other lets you change what happens: a deploy pipeline, a feature-flag service, a config file someone has to edit over SSH. When something goes wrong, you are the glue between them.

ACR (Application Control Room) puts both halves in one place. You observe what your apps are doing, and you change how they behave while they are running, from the same console, through the same SDK.

Two halves, one loop​

Observe​

Install an SDK and your app starts reporting:

  • Visitors and sessions, with pages, referrers, browsers, devices and countries, in Analytics.
  • Your own events, like checkout_started or invoice_paid, with attributes you can filter on, in Events.
  • Errors, grouped into issues with readable stack traces, in Errors.
  • Log lines with levels from debug to fatal, plus a live tail, in Logs.
  • Request timings and Core Web Vitals, with p50, p95 and p99, in Performance.
  • Server heartbeats: CPU, memory, event loop and garbage collection for each Node.js process.

Around that sit Uptime checks for your public URLs, PM2 process monitoring, Releases that track adoption and crash-free sessions per version, and Alerts that turn the signals you care about into incidents on email, Slack, Discord, Teams or a webhook.

Control​

This is the half most monitoring tools leave out. From Controls and Flags you can, without a deploy:

  • turn a feature flag on for your team, then 5%, then everyone, and off again in one click;
  • put the app into maintenance mode, with a message and a list of users who can still get in;
  • show an announcement banner to every visitor, now or on a schedule;
  • tell outdated tabs that a new version is available, or force them to reload;
  • clear stale caches and service workers in every open browser;
  • turn telemetry collection off entirely, sample it, or raise the minimum log level.

How it works​

Both halves travel over the same channel. The SDK batches telemetry and sends it to ACR. Separately, it polls one small, cached JSON document called the control snapshot, which holds everything you configured under Controls and Flags. SDKs check about once a minute and only download the snapshot when it changed.

That design has a few consequences you will feel right away:

  • No inbound ports. ACR never connects into your app. Your app always calls out, so there is nothing to expose or firewall.
  • Flags cost nothing to read. They are evaluated inside the SDK from the last snapshot, so checking a flag never makes a network request, and keeps working offline.
  • Changes land in about a minute, and immediately when a browser tab comes back into focus.

The rules ACR follows​

A few principles shape the whole product.

Everything belongs to an app. There are no workspaces, organizations or environments to configure. "Shop - production" and "Shop - staging" are two apps, each with its own keys, members and data. One app is one thing you deploy.

The credential decides the app. An SDK request never says which app it belongs to. ACR works that out from the API key, so nobody can write into another app by editing a payload. Browser keys only work from the origins you allow, and server secrets never go into a browser bundle; the browser SDK refuses them.

Telemetry must never hurt your app. SDK calls never throw. Buffers have a fixed size. Failed sends retry with backoff, and when ACR is busy it asks clients to slow down instead of falling over.

Privacy by default. Query strings are stripped, sensitive keys are redacted, email addresses in free text are masked, and visitor IP addresses are never stored.

What ACR is not (yet)​

Being clear about this saves you time:

  • It is not a distributed tracing backend. Trace and span ids travel with your logs and errors so you can filter by them, but ACR does not store traces or custom metrics.
  • It does not instrument every transport. HTTP is covered; GraphQL, WebSocket and microservice transports in NestJS are not instrumented automatically.
  • It is built for JavaScript first. There are SDKs for the browser, React, Angular, Node.js, Express and NestJS. Services in other languages can still send events through the public events API.

Who it is for​

ACR is for the people who ship a web app and then have to keep it healthy: small product teams without a dedicated operations group, developers who want to see their errors next to the switch that fixes them, and anyone who has ever wished they could turn a feature off faster than their deploy pipeline allows.

Try it​

Connect your first app takes a few minutes: create an app, add an API key, install an SDK and watch the first visitor arrive. Then open Controls, flip maintenance mode on, and watch your own app respond within a minute. That moment is the whole idea of ACR.
